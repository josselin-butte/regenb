For more than twenty-five years, I've been watching the regenerative movement across every sector. Its roots are many: permaculture, systems thinking, complexity thinking, biomimicry, bioregionalism, at the crossroads of the social and natural sciences. Some are deep roots. Others are passing stages, useful for a while to move collective thinking forward.

Today in France, robustness is everywhere. Many people treat it as the latest marvel, the movement that surpasses all the others. A recent conversation made me want to look more closely: where does it come from, what does it really say, and what place should it have?

:::essentiel
- **An old and solid scientific foundation.** Well before the current movement, biology, engineering, ecology and the study of the commons had established the same trade-off: a robust system is generally less efficient than an optimised one [13, 19, 20].
- **Olivier Hamant's own contribution is cultural and political.** He makes desirable the qualities that the race for performance looks down on: slowness, redundancy, diversity, inconsistency [2, 4].
- **Robustness isn't a moral quality.** A hostile army or a productivist industry can be extremely robust. The real question remains: robustness of what, against what, and for whom [17, 25]?
- **The word serves very different projects.** It's used by citizen learning communities as well as by organisations like The Shift Project, whose "robust plan" includes relaunching nuclear power [28, 29].
- **For a regenerative approach, it's necessary but not enough.** It lets a system hold. Regeneration aims to make the system evolve and to enrich its surroundings [22].
:::

## What Olivier Hamant says

A biologist at INRAE and director of the Institut Michel Serres, Olivier Hamant defines robustness as "the capacity of a system to maintain its stability (in the short term) and its viability (in the long term) despite fluctuations" [1]. He contrasts it with performance, which he breaks down into effectiveness (reaching the goal) and efficiency (with as few resources as possible) [3, 5].

His thesis fits into an often-quoted line: living beings "are not performant and robust, they are robust because they are not performant" [11]. Sub-optimality isn't a flaw. It leaves margins. Performance isn't banned for all that: it can be switched on occasionally, like a fever, but becomes dangerous if it lasts [5, 11].

In La Troisième Voie du vivant (The third way of the living, 2022), he details the useful "counter-performances": chance, redundancy, waste, heterogeneity, fluctuation, slowness, inconsistency, incompleteness [4]. At 37 °C, for example, our enzymes don't work at their maximum; mixtures of wheat varieties have a lower yield, but a more stable one [1].

He also distinguishes robustness from its neighbours. Resilience, he argues, has become an injunction to bounce back after taking the hit; "robustness creates the conditions in which you don't fall" [1, 5]. Agility, in his view, is acceleration in disguise [5]. And, he says, "the opposite of intoxication isn't sobriety, it's robustness" [10]. His later books extend the thinking to politics (De l'incohérence, 2024) and then to business (L'Entreprise robuste, 2025) [6, 8].

## Roots older than the movement

This is where the subject becomes solid. Olivier Hamant didn't invent the notion: he popularised it and drew social consequences from it. In the sciences, robustness has been studied for decades.

- **Canalisation (Waddington, 1940s).** An organism's development produces stable forms despite genetic and environmental variation [12].
- **Systems biology (Kitano, 2004).** Robustness maintains functions through control, back-up mechanisms and modularity. Kitano insists that it has a cost, and that it comes with extreme fragility to other disturbances [13].
- **Robustness and evolvability (Wagner).** The more robust a system, the more its variations stay silent, and those variations then become a reserve for innovation [14]. Well built, robustness doesn't freeze things.
- **Degeneracy (Edelman, Whitacre).** Different elements can fulfil the same function [15]. Systems made of identical copies evolve poorly; those made of partly versatile elements evolve much better [16]. This valuable nuance is rarely mentioned in the French debate.
- **"Robust yet fragile" (Carlson and Doyle, 2002).** Highly organised complex systems are robust to what is common or anticipated, but fragile to what is rare or unforeseen [17]. To me, this is the best antidote to a naïve view of robustness.
- **Requisite variety (Ashby).** "Only variety can absorb variety": this is the cybernetic foundation for praising diversity [18].
- **The window of vitality (Ulanowicz, Goerner, Lietaer, 2009).** In flow networks, sustainability depends on a balance: "in general, more efficiency means less resilience" [19]. The message isn't "less performance" but "neither too much nor too little".
- **The commons (Ostrom).** In 2004, Anderies, Janssen and Ostrom proposed a framework for analysing the robustness of social-ecological systems, and pointed out that a robust system is generally less efficient than its non-robust equivalent [20].

The regenerative field already knew many of these intuitions. In the principles of permaculture, David Holmgren writes that a system made of autonomous elements is more robust to disturbance, and sees diversity as insurance against hazards [21]. More recently, Sylvain Breuzard and Olivier Hamant brought together the "permaentreprise" (permaculture applied to business) and robustness around eight levers [33].

Carol Sanford, for her part, places robustness below regeneration: whatever is resilient, restored, robust or sustainable withstands shocks or recovers from them, and stays the same; a regenerative business comes out of shocks better [22]. This echoes Nassim Taleb's distinction, for whom robustness isn't antifragility [23].

## Why it's succeeding now

The word's success comes from the meeting of a legitimate scientific figure, an era of crises one after another (Covid, the Suez Canal blockage, cyberattacks, megafires) and a weariness with "resilience" [34]. That weariness is well founded: drawing on Fukushima, Thierry Ribault describes resilience as "a technology of consent", which makes people accept disaster instead of fighting its causes [24].

The word has become a platform shared by very different players: learning communities on robustness [32], local areas, businesses, consultancies. The Shift Project uses it in an engineer's sense, "fulfilling its functions despite hazards", in a plan that includes relaunching nuclear power [28, 29], while Olivier Hamant criticises the ITER project, for example [34]. The same word therefore means a philosophy of sub-optimality for one, and a risk analysis of decarbonisation pathways for the other.

## Limits to know about

**Moving from nature to society.** This is the most serious criticism. Observations about living things aren't enough to ground political rules. In a criticism reported by Causeur, Thomas Lepeltier points out that the robustness we admire in nature is the product of a ruthless mechanism dominated by extinction [26]. Hayek also invoked nature to justify the market [4].

**Robustness isn't good in itself.** Vincent Mignerot criticises Olivier Hamant for moralising a property that comes from evolution, which has no purpose [25]. In science, nobody talks about robustness without specifying the function maintained and the disturbance considered [17, 20].

**Too sharp an opposition.** For Jean Pariès, a specialist in resilience engineering, the literature on complex systems already describes most of the mechanisms presented in L'Entreprise robuste; he disputes the opposition between performance and robustness, and points out that organisations are shot through with power relations [7]. Systems sciences speak of balance rather than opposition [13, 19].

**The fashion effect.** After agility, resilience and frugality, some wonder whether robustness isn't "the new fashionable corporate word" [27]. An irony to watch: turning it into numerical indicators [30] can bring back Goodhart's law, which Olivier Hamant himself denounces: a measure that becomes a target stops being reliable [3].

**Rigour in the examples.** Olivier Hamant cites a photosynthetic yield below 1% [3], whereas the article by Arp and colleagues that he discusses describes light harvesting with an efficiency close to 100% [31]. The two figures don't measure the same thing. In training, it's better to say which yield you mean.

## What place to give it

Here's my reading, open to discussion. I distinguish three layers.

- **A solid root, to keep and to teach with its sources:** the trade-off between efficiency and margins, the role of variability, redundancy and versatility, requisite variety, Ostrom's principles, robustness in the service of the capacity to evolve.
- **A useful cultural contribution, as a way in with leaders and groups:** overturning the cult of performance, and giving legitimacy to slowness, disagreement and inconsistency.
- **A passing trend, to welcome with discernment:** the binary opposition between performance and robustness, the war of words with resilience, the management label, robustness raised to a supreme value.

Robustness is a real scientific root and a welcome correction to the cult of performance. It becomes a fashion as soon as it's made a supreme value rather than a property to be situated and put in the service of a broader project.

:::accompagnement
**Five questions before using the word with a group**

- **Robustness of what?** Which function or relationship do we want to maintain [17]?
- **Against what?** Which fluctuations are expected, and which haven't been thought about? Being robust to the expected can make you fragile to the unforeseen [17].
- **For whom, and at whose expense?** A system's stability can be paid for by the exhaustion of some of its members or of its surroundings [25].
- **With what room to evolve?** Prefer partial versatility to simple duplicates, to keep the capacity to change [16].
- **In the service of what project?** Robustness lets you hold; regeneration helps the system and its surroundings grow [22].

In practice, in facilitation and governance, this looks like simple choices: back up critical roles with versatility rather than copies, keep time margins in session plans, make disagreement a resource, prefer a few simple rules to a long rulebook [9], and start from the "robustness already there" in a team or a local area [35].
:::

If I had to say it in one sentence: I look for systems robust enough to hold through fluctuations, diverse enough to keep learning, and connected enough to their surroundings to regenerate them.
