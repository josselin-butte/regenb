We converted our conventional toilet into a dry toilet. The reason fitted in one sentence: to stop peeing and pooing in drinking water.

My wife couldn't wait for it to be done; she was genuinely enthusiastic. I wanted to understand what we were changing. Because behind a flush there's a whole system: pipes, treatment plants, rivers, fields, and a history going back to the nineteenth century. Here's what I found.

:::essentiel
- **Flushing accounts for about 20% of a French household's drinking water,** close to 10,000 litres per person per year [1, 2].
- **Our excrement is rich.** Urine holds about 85% of the nitrogen and 65% of the phosphorus we excrete [3]. Today in France, only about 10% of excreted nitrogen goes back to the fields [4].
- **The debate isn't new.** As early as 1862, Victor Hugo denounced Paris as a "leaking basket" throwing its fertiliser into the river [6]. The 1894 law on mains drainage decided otherwise [7].
- **It's legal in France,** under conditions: no nuisance to neighbours, no liquid discharge outside the property, no water pollution [8].
- **For a group, it's a small lesson in change:** a simple reason, one person driving it, a fear that dissolves with use, and learning by doing.
:::

## Drinking water to carry our waste

In France, toilets account for about 20% of domestic water use: 27 litres per person per day, close to 10,000 litres a year [1, 2]. That's water treated to be drunk, which we use to flush away a few hundred grams of matter.

The standard system rests on three parts: flush toilets, mains sewers and treatment plants. It produces about 150 litres of wastewater per person per day [3]. Treatment plants then remove what they can before returning the water to the river, using solutions that are "costly in equipment, chemical reagents and energy", and releasing nitrous oxide, a powerful greenhouse gas [3].

The result is only partial. In the Paris region, a third of the nitrogen is still released into the Seine, the equivalent of the discharge of four million people, and water quality thresholds are exceeded downstream of Paris [3].

## What we're really throwing away

Our excrement isn't just waste. It's nutrients that plants gave us, by way of our plates.

- **Urine** makes up about 1% of the volume of wastewater, but it holds most of the nitrogen and phosphorus we excrete [3, 9]. It generally carries very few pathogens [3].
- **Faeces** are small in volume (100 to 150 g a day), but that's where the pathogens are [9]. They're what really needs sanitary treatment.

In the Paris region, only 4% of the nitrogen and 41% of the phosphorus in our excrement is put to use in farming today, through sewage sludge [3]. Across the country, about 10% of excreted nitrogen is recycled [4]. The rest is lost, into water or air. Meanwhile, farming buys synthetic fertiliser, and the European Union lists phosphate rock among its critical raw materials [5].

The researcher Fabien Esculier, who has led the OCAPI programme at the École des Ponts since 2014, works on closing this loop. At first the subject seemed "very original, not to say incongruous"; eight years later, farmers and local authorities were taking it seriously [10]. Today he argues that it's possible to do without synthetic fertilisers [11].

## An old story

In 1862, in Les Misérables, Victor Hugo devoted a chapter to the sewers of Paris, "the intestine of Leviathan". He wrote: "Paris throws twenty-five millions into the water every year." For him, "the most fertilising and the most effective of manures is human manure", and sending everything to the river leaves "the land impoverished and the water poisoned" [6].

Thirty years later, on 10 July 1894, a law required cesspit waste to be discharged into the Paris sewers. Prefect Eugène Poubelle pushed it through against the opposition of Pasteur and other hygienist scientists [7]. The aim was legitimate: to put an end to epidemics. Even so, it took time for the link between the city and the fields to come undone. At the beginning of the twentieth century, the Paris area was still recycling half the nitrogen and 70% of the phosphorus from its excrement into farming, through land spreading [3].

What strikes me is that our current toilets aren't self-evident. They're the result of a collective choice, made at a given time, for good reasons. Other choices are still possible.

## What the law says

In France, dry toilets are permitted by article 17 of the decree of 7 September 2009, amended in 2012 [8]. The conditions are simple to state:

- **no nuisance to neighbours, no liquid discharge outside the property, no pollution** of surface or ground water;
- **a watertight container**, emptied regularly onto a watertight area sheltered from the weather;
- **compost used on the property**;
- **household greywater** (shower, sink, washing machine) is still treated by a compliant system [8].

A ministerial answer from 2011 specifies that these toilets are allowed "including in areas with mains sewerage", as long as the rules are followed [2]. The local public service for non-mains sanitation (SPANC) is the body to consult [2]. Collecting urine separately to spread it on land, however, still sits in an uncertain regulatory framework [16].

## What surprised us

Three things, in our experience.

**The quantity.** Each of us produces between 1 and 1.5 litres of urine a day [9]. With a flush, everything disappears. With a dry toilet, you see what you produce, and you become aware of the volumes.

**The simplicity.** A bucket, some dry material, a compost heap. No complicated mechanism.

**No smell.** That was the main fear, and it never materialised. Research says the same: a well-designed, well-used dry toilet doesn't smell bad; the smell of ammonia mainly appears when urine sits too long [9].

## Limits to know about

I don't want to present this as a miracle solution.

- **Compost takes time and attention.** Two years of maturing is generally recommended for compost from sawdust toilets [9]. The World Health Organization sets storage guidelines of six to twenty-four months depending on conditions before agricultural use [13].
- **Vegetable gardens call for caution.** Some guides, such as the one from the Nice Côte d'Azur metropolitan area, advise against using this compost on food crops if composting hasn't been perfectly controlled [14]. In a cohousing project studied by researchers, residents chose to spread it at the foot of birch trees rather than in the vegetable garden [12].
- **It takes learning.** Managing moisture, smells and small flies comes through trial and error [12, 9].
- **It isn't possible everywhere.** In a flat with no garden or lift, the constraints of space and transport become significant [12]. Using all of a family's urine also requires land [9].
- **Some questions remain open,** in particular about medicine residues in urine [9].

The most important change probably happens at the scale of neighbourhoods. In Paris, the future Saint-Vincent-de-Paul eco-district plans separate urine collection, turned into fertiliser for the city's green spaces, about 47 m³ a year [15]. On the Saclay plateau, the public development agency is testing source separation in a first building [10].

## What it teaches me about change

I hadn't planned to write about toilets in a facilitator's notebook. And yet this small domestic transformation looks a lot like what the teams I work with go through.

:::accompagnement
**What our toilets taught me about change**

- **A simple, shared reason.** "Stop doing our business in drinking water": everyone understands, nobody needs a long argument. What's the simple sentence behind your change?
- **One person driving it.** My wife's enthusiasm made taking the step obvious. In a group, who carries the momentum, and how can the others lean on it?
- **The fear we anticipate is often bigger than the experience.** We were afraid of the smell; it never came. Which fears could you test on a small scale rather than debate?
- **Making visible what was hidden.** A flush hides what we produce. A dry toilet shows it, and that changes how you see things. What does your organisation flush away without seeing it?
- **Keep a way back.** Researchers observed that a reversible solution, one that lets you go back to a flush toilet if there's a problem, "will reassure the most hesitant" [12]. What safety net would make your trial easier?
- **Learning by doing.** You don't learn compost from a book. You have to try, observe and adjust, together.
:::

This note is still "exploring": our first compost isn't mature yet. I'll come back and add to it once it has fed the garden.
