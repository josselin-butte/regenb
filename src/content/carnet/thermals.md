In paragliding, you don't fly thanks to an engine, because there isn't one. You fly thanks to something you can't see: warm air rising. The best you can do is feel it, guess where it is, settle into it without forcing, and leave it at the right moment.

I find this very close to what I look for when I work with a group. In a room, a circle or a team, there's an energy that rises and falls. You can't command it, but you can learn to read it. I wanted to check whether the intuition held, so I went to see what atmospheric physicists, ornithologists and safety specialists have to say.

:::essentiel
- **A thermal can't be commanded, only read.** It's born from heated ground, rises in intermittent bubbles, and depends on the time of day and the terrain [1, 2].
- **Great soaring birds barely flap their wings.** The Andean condor flaps for just 1% of its flight time, mostly at take-off and at the end of glides, close to the ground [3].
- **Together, we read the air better.** Among storks, some explore and others follow; both roles have a cost and a use [4].
- **The decision to take off is often collective, and full of traps.** Familiarity, the wish to be accepted, commitment to a goal or the presence of other groups all push us to take more risks [7, 8].
- **For a facilitator, the lesson is simple:** look for the lift that's already there, only push at key moments, and know when to give up.
:::

## An energy you can't see

A thermal is born from ground warming in the sun. The air just above it heats up, expands, becomes lighter than the air around it, then breaks away and rises [1]. As it climbs, it draws in surrounding air and mixes with it. It finally slows under a more stable layer, the inversion, where it spreads out; if the air is humid enough, a cumulus cloud forms at its top [1].

The researcher Sergio Lambertucci compares thermals to lava lamps: bubbles of air rising intermittently when the ground is warm enough. A bird can arrive at the right place, but at the wrong time [3]. The griffon vultures of the Pyrenees know this: they often only take off in late morning, once rising currents have formed in the warmed air [2].

I find the same thing in workshops. A group's energy isn't constant. It comes in waves. It depends on the time of day, on what happened before, on the ground you're standing on. Arriving with the best exercise at the wrong moment is like taking off before the air will carry you.

## The art of not flapping

In 2020, a team of researchers fitted eight Andean condors with flight recorders. The result: they flapped their wings for only 1% of their flight time. More than three quarters of those wingbeats happened at take-off. One bird flew for five hours without flapping, covering about 172 kilometres [3, 5]. The condors also flapped more at the end of glides between two thermals, as they got closer to the ground [3].

Griffon vultures do the same: they circle in a thermal to gain height, then glide in a straight line to the next one, covering several hundred kilometres in a few hours with minimal energy [2].

I see a valuable clue here for anyone who supports groups. A facilitator's effort matters most at two moments: take-off, when a group needs launching, and transitions, when the energy drops between two high points. The rest of the time, the group carries itself. Flapping all the time tires everyone out, and stops you sensing what is really rising.

## Reading the signs

You can't see a thermal, but it leaves clues: a cumulus forming, a bird circling, another pilot climbing. Griffon vultures also use each other's behaviour: when one dives towards the ground, the others understand it has spotted something [2]. The researcher Hannah Williams wonders whether birds that depend on air currents watch each other's flight to, in a sense, map the air around them [3].

In a group, the signs are of the same kind. Someone sitting up straighter, a silence that changes texture, a laugh that travels, a question that keeps coming back. None of them is enough on its own. Together, they draw the map of the air.

## Flying together in the same thermal

When several pilots share a thermal, a few simple rules make flying possible. The first to arrive sets the direction of rotation, and everyone turns the same way. The lowest pilot has priority, because they can't see well above them. And to join a circle, you enter it tangentially, without cutting across anyone's path [6].

White storks tell us even more. In 2018, a team from the Max Planck Institute used GPS to follow a flock of 27 storks on migration [4]. Two strategies emerged:

- **The leaders** spot distant thermals, head for them, then look for the best areas by constantly moving in and out of the lift.
- **The followers** watch the leaders and place themselves directly in the areas that rise best. But between two thermals, they have to flap much more to keep up with the group [4].

In the long run, the followers migrated less far: many stopped in Spain, while most of the leaders reached Africa [4]. For the researcher Andrea Flack, interactions between birds a few metres apart end up shaping an entire migration [4].

It's a beautiful image of complexity thinking: a global order emerging from local interactions, with no conductor. It's also a lesson about roles. In a team, those who explore take risks and pay a cost. Those who follow benefit from their work, but wear themselves out if they never find their own lift. A living group needs both, and gains from making those roles visible.

## Take off, or give up

The most delicate moment in paragliding is often played out on the ground, at launch: go, or not. The French free-flight federation is clear about it: accident factors combine, and you have to ask whether you can fly "today, in these conditions, with my level, my condition", and "know how to give up" [9]. It also flags, in two words, the "group effect: possible danger!" [9].

The researcher Ian McCammon studied hundreds of avalanche accidents and identified decision traps that show up in every mountain sport [7, 8]:

- **Familiarity:** "I know this spot, it'll be fine."
- **The wish to be accepted** by the group.
- **Commitment:** "We said we'd go today."
- **The expert halo:** following the most experienced person without asking your own questions.
- **Scarcity:** "If we don't do it today, it'll be too late."
- **Other groups being there:** "They're going, so it must be fine."

Aviation knows a related trap, plan continuation bias: carrying on with the plan when the available information says you should abandon it. It gets stronger the closer you get to the goal. The US Federal Aviation Administration insists that diverting should be seen as a success, not a failure [10].

These traps don't stay at the launch site. I find them in leadership teams, project teams and the groups I work with, every time a group decides under uncertainty. Naming them out loud, before the decision, often changes the quality of the conversation.

In paragliding, and in other disciplines, there's a saying: when in doubt, there is no doubt. Every time I'm unsure about flying, I don't go. I'm not an excellent pilot, and so far: zero accidents ☺

## Where the image stops

A group isn't a mass of air. People have intentions, a history, power relationships. A metaphor sheds light but proves nothing, and I've already written in this notebook how wary we should be of moving straight from nature to society. So I take thermals for what they are: a way of looking that helps me slow down and read better what's going on.

:::accompagnement
**Seven questions I ask myself before working with a group**

- **Where is the lift today?** What signs show me what's already carrying this group?
- **Is this the right moment?** Should I wait for the air to warm up, like the vultures in late morning?
- **Where do I really need to flap?** At the start, in the transitions, and as little as possible elsewhere.
- **Who explores, who follows, and who pays the cost?** How can I make these roles visible and rotate them?
- **How do I join the circle without cutting across it?** Joining a conversation already under way means following its direction before proposing your own.
- **What's pushing us to take off anyway?** Familiarity, commitment, the wish to be accepted, other people being there: which one is at work?
- **Could giving up be a success?** If the air isn't carrying, landing earlier is sometimes the best decision of the day.
:::
