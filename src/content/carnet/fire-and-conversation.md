## A summer without fire

The summer of 2026 was a succession of heatwaves. By late August, more than 80% of the country was under water-use restrictions [2]. By late September, the French national forestry office counted about 21,000 fire outbreaks and 98,000 hectares burned in mainland France, five times the average of the last twenty years [1]. The largest fire, in Gironde, swept through 42,000 hectares and destroyed around 180 houses at Le Porge [1].

In Haute-Garonne, a decree of 9 July banned, during very high risk alerts, bivouacking and the use of fire, stoves included, in forest areas [3]. During our four days of walking in the Comminges, we did not light a fire.

The evenings were beautiful. But something was missing, and it wasn't the warmth: it was still thirty degrees at nightfall. What was missing was a centre. Without a fire, everyone looks at their phone, their map, their feet. We still talk, but we talk about tomorrow: the next stage, the water, the mules.

## By day, people sort things out; by night, they tell stories

In 1974, anthropologist Polly Wiessner spent two months recording the conversations of the Ju/'hoansi, a people of the Kalahari. She compared 174 day and night conversations [4]. By day, 34% of exchanges were complaints, criticism and gossip, which serve to regulate relationships, 31% dealt with practical matters like hunting, and barely 6% were stories [5]. In the evening, around the fire, more than 80% of conversations were stories [6].

These are the same people, in the same group, but the light has changed, and so has the speech. For Wiessner, these evening stories pass on what is known about absent people, neighbours and ancestors, and sustain trust far beyond the small group [4].

Psychologist Robin Dunbar draws a strong idea from this. By pushing back the night, fire lengthened the active day, perhaps by up to four hours. That gained time was not used to work more: it was used for conversation, stories, songs and dances — in other words, for bonding [6].

I see exactly this in seminars. Decisions get made during the day, often well. But what makes a group trust each other happens in the evening, when nobody has anything left to sort out.

## A fire at the centre

Circle practitioners Christina Baldwin and Ann Linnea open their method with a sentence: "Once upon a time, fire led our ancestors into the circle." It made sense to put the fire in the middle and sit around it [7]. When I place an object at the centre of a talking circle — a candle, a stone, a bowl of water — I'm not decorating. I'm giving a centre back to people who would otherwise be looking at each other as if in a negotiation.

The Pyrenees know another collective fire. Every year, on the night of the summer solstice, residents come down from the peaks with torches to light great fires built according to tradition. UNESCO listed these festivals as intangible cultural heritage in 2015 [8]. The descent is a rite of passage for the young, from adolescence to adulthood. The first fire is sometimes lit by the mayor, sometimes by the village's most recently married man. In the morning, people gather embers or ashes to protect houses and gardens [8].

In Aran and the Comminges, these fires had almost disappeared during the Second World War and the years that followed. They came back with those who returned to the land [9]. This year, the tradition came back to the village where we live.

A village that lights a fire together again is not folklore. It is a collective reminding itself that it exists.

## Good fire… and bad fire

Historian Stephen Pyne distinguishes three fires: natural fire, the fire that human animals learned to manage, and the burning of fossil fuels. We live, he says, in the Pyrocene, the age of the fire we created. His conclusion fits in one sentence: "Good fire made us. Bad fire could unmake us." [11]

The Pyrenees keep a trace of this good fire: écobuage, controlled pastoral burning. It serves to maintain the summer pastures by limiting scrub. In the Hautes-Pyrénées, it has been supervised since the 1990s by local commissions. It is reserved for livestock farmers, allowed only from November to April, declared in advance, and it requires constant watching until extinction [10].

I see in it a fair image of a group's energy. Fire, in a collective, is momentum, passion, sometimes anger. It is not a problem to put out. But a useful fire is a tended fire: a season, a perimeter, someone who watches until the end. A conflict opened at the end of the day, with no one staying until it dies down, looks like a fire lit on a windy day.

## What I do with this in workshops

Wiessner asks a question that stays with me. What happens when artificial light turns this "economically unproductive" evening time into productive time [4]?

I see what happens in seminars: we fill the evenings. One last workshop, a debrief, a "friendly" evening with an entertainer. We turn night into a prolonged day. And then we're surprised that the group produced a lot without ever truly meeting.

So when I design a multi-day seminar, I separate the two times. By day, we decide. In the evening, we decide nothing. We tell stories: where we come from, what brought us here, a story about someone who isn't in the room. And I don't facilitate: I keep the centre.

## The limits of the image

Wiessner's data come from a single society, observed in 1974. They don't prove that all fires produce stories, nor that all stories need a fire. A fire doesn't make a group safe: you can also exclude, humiliate or lie by firelight.

And after a summer like this one, I'm wary of any nostalgia for campfires. Fire destroyed homes, forests and lives. So I take fire for what it is here: a way of looking at what happens when a group stops solving problems and starts telling stories.

:::accompagnement
**Six questions I ask myself before a collective moment**

- **Where is the centre?** What are people looking at together, other than each other?
- **Is this a time for sorting or for telling?** Have I mixed the two into the same hour?
- **Have I protected the evening?** Or have I filled it with one more workshop?
- **Who lights, who carries, who watches?** As with the brandons, whose is each role, and is it said out loud?
- **Is this fire tended?** If a conflict opens, is there a season, a perimeter and someone to stay until it dies down?
- **What stories circulate about the absent?** They build trust, or they undo it.
:::
